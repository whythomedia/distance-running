The plan

Eat in three small pieces, not one big one

Each piece clears your stomach before the next one lands. The last one hits your blood right when you need it.

4:15

Bagel with jelly

At the kitchen table. Nothing else. No eggs, no cheese, no bacon.

Not feeling a bagel?
  • Packet of instant oatmeal with honey
  • White rice with a pinch of salt and maple syrup
  • Two slices of white toast with jelly
  • Plain waffle or pancakes with syrup, no butter
  • Banana and a handful of pretzels

Same rule every time: mostly carb, almost no fat, almost no fiber.

6:00

Fruit smoothie, sipped on the bus

Banana, a little frozen fruit, juice or water, honey, pinch of salt. No yogurt, no milk, no protein powder. Insulated bottle so it's still cold.

Not feeling a smoothie?
  • 20 oz bottle of Gatorade, sipped over the ride
  • Applesauce pouch and a banana
  • Two fig bars and water
  • A squeezie and a small handful of pretzels

Anything that sips or goes down in two bites. Nothing that needs a fork.

7:40

Squeezies, then water only

About 45 minutes before the gun. After this, sips of water and nothing else.

Not feeling a squeezie?
  • A handful of energy chews or gummy candy
  • Half a banana
  • 8 oz of Gatorade
  • A honey packet

Pure sugar, small, and done by 7:45.

Two rules that outrank everything

Rehearse it. Run this exact sequence before a hard Tuesday workout. Race day is never the first rep.

Burrito rides home in the cooler. It's not a bad breakfast. It's a great lunch. Timing is the only problem.

Why it works

How you're powered

Almost everything with calories in it is one of . Your body does something different with each one, and only one of them runs a race.

Fast fuel

Bread, rice, fruit, pasta, honey, sugar. Your body breaks these down into fast. Burns hot, burns quick.

Slow fuel

Bacon, cheese, butter, oil. Huge tank, tiny pump. Great for a six hour hike. Useless for 20 minutes at threshold.

Building material

Eggs, meat, whey. Not fuel at all. Bricks, not gas. You don't race on it, you rebuild with it afterward.

The battery

Here's the part that matters. When you eat carbs, your body turns them into blood sugar, and then packs whatever it doesn't need right now into storage inside your muscles and liver. That stored form is called . Think of it as a battery.

Glycogencarbs, stored in your legs, ready to go

A 5K runs almost entirely off that battery plus whatever sugar is already in your blood. The battery gets charged over the days before the race, mostly from dinner the night before. Race morning can't fill it. Race morning can only top it off and keep the stomach out of the way.

Fat is the backup generator. It's always running a little, but it can't spin up fast enough to matter in a race this short.

But what about the Bro-Tein

+

People talk a lot about protein. They're not wrong. Protein is critical for building and keeping muscle, and for people who aren't racing, it's usually the macro they're short on. That's why Dad eats protein-heavy and keeps carbs low: at 42, not running 5Ks, the goal is holding onto muscle and staying lean.

You are the opposite case on race morning. You already built the muscle. What you need is the charge, and protein doesn't charge the battery. Same food rules, different job, different answer. Protein is for the ride home.

Where food actually goes

The pipeline

Food doesn't become fuel the second you swallow it. It moves through stages, and each stage takes time, more or less depending on what you ate and what your body is busy doing. The whole question of race morning is: where is your breakfast at 8:30?

1

Stomach

A holding tank. It churns food and releases it downstream a little at a time. That release rate is called . Carbs and liquids clear in about 1 to 2 hours. Fat takes 3 to 5. Anything still in here at the gun is dead weight that sloshes.

2

Small intestine

The loading dock. Nutrients cross into your blood here. That's . Carbs are in your blood within about an hour of leaving the stomach. Fat takes several more hours, and it doesn't show up as usable race fuel anyway.

3

Blood

Sugar in the blood is available now. This is what the squeezies are for: it skips the slow parts and lands here fast, right before the gun.

4

Legs

Muscles pull from blood sugar and from their own glycogen battery. At the gun, your body also away from your gut and toward your legs. Digestion basically pauses. Whatever's left in the stomach just sits there for the next 20 minutes.

Three brakes on the whole system

How fast everything above moves is called . Race morning hits the brakes three times.

What we learned, in one breath

  • Only carbs run a 5K. Fat is too slow, protein isn't fuel.
  • Glycogen is the battery. Filled yesterday, topped off this morning.
  • Fat hits the brakes on gastric emptying, so a burrito is still in your stomach at the gun.
  • 4 AM and nerves slow everything further. Every timeline stretches.
  • Small carb pieces, spaced out, keep the stomach light and the blood sugar steady.

Bonus

Why humans are weirdly good at this

Almost any animal your size beats you in a sprint. A house cat beats you. But stretch the distance out and the list of animals that can stay with a fit human on a warm day gets very short, and past about 20 miles it's basically empty. Distance running isn't something humans do in spite of our bodies. It's what the body was built for.

The hardware

  • We , everywhere. Most mammals cool by panting, which stops working once they're running hard. We shed heat through millions of sweat glands on bare skin while running full speed. Heat, not fatigue, is what stops most animals.
  • Springs in the legs. The Achilles tendon and the arch of the foot store energy on landing and give it back on push-off. Chimps, our closest relatives, have almost no Achilles and can't run far at all.
  • Breathing on our own schedule. A four-legged animal's lungs are squeezed by its own gallop, one breath per stride, no choice. Standing upright freed our breathing from our stride, so we can take in air at whatever rate the pace demands.
  • Big glutes, long legs, a ligament to hold the head still. None of these matter for walking. All of them matter for running. They all show up in the fossil record about the same time.

Why it mattered

Long before spears were any good, people hunted by . Not faster, just longer. Pick an antelope on a hot day, keep it moving, never let it rest in the shade, and in a few hours it overheats and stops. The human, sweating and carrying water, jogs up to it. Some groups still hunt this way.

Which brings this back to the bagel. All of that hardware only works if two systems are fed: the muscles need fuel and the skin needs blood for cooling. Your body is juggling both the whole race, and it can't afford to have a third system, a stomach full of bacon, competing for the same supply. Race morning eating is just making sure the machine you were born with gets to do its thing.
